The shape of it

Every design here is the same five stages. What changes between a small setup and Cloudflare is where the money goes and what happens when a stage falls behind.

What people actually ask it

Start here, because the queries decide the storage and nothing else does.

  • One request went wrong. Find every line carrying this trace id. A needle in a very large haystack, almost always in the last few hours.
  • Something is broken right now. Count errors by service and version over five minutes. An aggregate, narrow and recent.
  • Tell me when this happens again. The same aggregate, evaluated on a schedule forever.
  • Who accessed what in March. Rare, wide, old, and usually a legal requirement rather than an engineering one.

The first three are most of the reads. The fourth is most of the bytes. A system that treats all four the same is either too slow for the first three or too expensive for the fourth.

Sizing it first

The arithmetic decides the architecture, so do it before choosing anything. Take a mid-size setup: 400 services, 4,000 instances, 50 lines a second each at 400 bytes a line.

For the top end, Cloudflare's logging pipeline moves close to a million log lines a second, and their wider analytics pipeline was ingesting 706 million events a second and 107 GiB/s of compressed data as of December 2024. That is roughly 100x their 2018 figure, doubling about every 18 months.

Two things follow. Even at mid-size, per-line overhead is a real cost, so anything you do per line has to be cheap. And whatever number you plan for will double, so everything wants to scale sideways.

Getting a line off the box

Three options, and the tradeoff is who suffers when the backend is slow.

  • In-process library, shipping directly. Simple, and the worst failure mode. Your service now has a hard dependency on the log backend being up and fast.
  • Sidecar per pod. Good isolation, and you pay for the container once per pod.
  • Node agent reading everyone's output. One process per machine, which is where most people end up.

The usual path is stdout to the container runtime to a file, with an agent tailing it. The wrinkle worth knowing: on systemd hosts the journal is a single-threaded bottleneck, and a very chatty service will hit it. Cloudflare has their agent listen on a Unix domain socket for high-throughput services so those bypass journald entirely.

They also replaced syslog-ng with the OpenTelemetry Collector, and the reasons are worth reading because none of them are throughput. It is written in Go rather than C, so more of their engineers can work on it. It was easier to build against their post-quantum crypto libraries. It reports Prometheus metrics about itself. And it let them run one agent for logs and traces instead of two. Operability was the whole argument.

Backpressure, and what to drop

This is the section that matters. When the pipeline cannot keep up, something gives, and the design is your choice about what.

A bounded local disk buffer is the primitive that earns its keep. It absorbs a queue outage without involving the application, and it survives the agent being restarted underneath it. Memory-only buffering dies with the process, which is exactly when you wanted the logs.

The noisy neighbour

The failure that actually happens is not the pipeline falling over. It is one service shipping a bad build, emitting fifty times its usual volume, and filling the shared pipe so that everyone else's logs are delayed or dropped. That happens at exactly the moment those logs matter.

Cloudflare's answer is max-min fair share. Streams inside their allocation get through untouched, and the ones over it share whatever is left. The service having a bad day degrades itself rather than the platform.

Sampling that does not lie

If you sample, every event has to carry the rate it was kept at, so counts can be scaled back up and you can put a confidence interval on an estimate. Cloudflare stores the sample interval per event and uses a Horvitz-Thompson estimator for sums and averages.

There is a subtlety here that cost them a real bug, and it is the best thing in any of this to remember.

Systematic sampling, keeping every nth event, looks equivalent to random sampling. It is not, when arrival order correlates with what you are measuring. Their responses arrive in bursts where the large ones come first, because a full HTML page is served before the small cached assets that follow it. Taking every nth event kept the large responses disproportionately, and size estimates came out high.

The queue in the middle

A queue between collection and storage decouples how fast logs are produced from how fast they can be indexed. Kafka is the default answer and a good one.

Retention is the number to think hardest about, because it is your outage budget. Cloudflare keeps enough to tolerate around eight hours of consumer downtime with no data loss. Pick it from how long you would realistically need to fix a broken consumer on a bad night, then add margin, because the bad night is when you find out.

Partitioning is the other decision. You want related lines together and load spread evenly, and Cloudflare partitions on host and service name. Note what that costs them: the syslog protocol carries only microsecond timestamps, so a service emitting faster than that has no reliable ordering, and ordering becomes best effort rather than a guarantee. Worth knowing before you promise anyone ordered logs.

Parse, enrich, redact

  • Parse. Structured at the source beats regex after the fact. If services emit JSON with consistent field names you avoid an entire category of problem. Grok patterns over unstructured text are where these pipelines rot.
  • Enrich. Attach what you will want to filter on later: region, cluster, service version, deploy id. The deploy id is what turns "errors went up" into "errors went up in the build that shipped at 14:20".
  • Redact. PII comes out here, before storage. Redacting at query time means the raw data is already sitting in your index, which is not a position you want to defend in an audit.

Storage and the index tradeoff

Three families, and the whole difference is how much index you build on write.

ApproachIndexesStored for 100 GB/day rawWeak at
Inverted index
Elasticsearch, OpenSearch		 every token ~500 GB write throughput, storage cost
Label index
Loki		 labels only ~30 GB plus a small index high cardinality, ad hoc search
Columnar
ClickHouse, Parquet on S3		 sparse, sorted ~30 to 50 GB true full text, schema drift

The industry has moved. Cloudflare ran a 90-node Elasticsearch cluster and has been migrating to ClickHouse, and Uber went the same way. The logic is that log queries are mostly filter-then-aggregate over recent data, which is what columnar engines are built for, while a full-text inverted index charges you on every write for a generality most log queries never use.

Cardinality

The failure specific to label-indexed systems, and easy to walk into.

A label with unbounded values creates a separate stream per value. Put a request id or a user id in a label and one stream becomes millions, the index stops being small, and the only reason you chose the system in the first place is gone.

The rule that holds: labels are for things you filter by that have a bounded set of values. Service, environment, level, region. Everything else belongs in the line body, where finding it costs a scan instead of an index entry.

Retention tiering

Most of your bytes are old and most of your reads are recent, so one storage tier is either too expensive or too slow. The inversion is the whole argument for tiering.

Set retention per class rather than globally. Debug logs at seven days and audit logs at seven years travel the same pipeline and are completely different products, priced differently and read differently.

How it fails

  • It logs into itself. If the logging pipeline ships its own logs through the logging pipeline, an outage takes out the tooling you would use to diagnose the outage. It needs a separate, dumber path for its own telemetry.
  • Volume spikes during incidents. Errors multiply log lines, so demand peaks exactly when you most need the system. Capacity planned for the average is capacity planned for the wrong day.
  • Thundering herd on recovery. A network partition heals and every agent flushes its disk buffer at once. Jitter the retry, or the recovery becomes the second outage.
  • Clock skew. "The last five minutes" is ambiguous across a fleet with drifting clocks. Store event time and ingest time separately and know which one each query means.
  • Silent parse failures. A line that fails to parse and gets dropped is invisible by definition. Count them, expose the count, and alert on the rate rather than the total.
